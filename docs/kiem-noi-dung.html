Bài

We tend to speak of forgetting as a failure, a small betrayal by a brain that cannot be trusted to hold on to what matters. We apologise for forgetting names, worry when we cannot recall why we walked into a room, and spend considerable sums on apps and courses that promise to sharpen our memories. Rarely do we stop to ask whether forgetting might be doing something useful, or whether a mind that retained everything would be any better off. There is, in fact, a famous case that suggests it would not. In the early twentieth century, a Russian journalist came to the attention of the psychologist Alexander Luria because he appeared to remember everything. He could reproduce long tables of numbers years after seeing them once, and recall the exact words of conversations he had barely attended to. Yet his extraordinary memory was far from a blessing. He struggled to follow the meaning of a story because every word called up a cascade of images, and he found it difficult to recognise faces, which change from day to day, because he remembered each version too precisely to see what they had in common. To understand, it seems, we must be able to let details go. This insight lies at the heart of a growing body of research which treats forgetting not as decay but as an active process. Some neuroscientists now argue that the brain spends considerable energy weakening the connections that hold memories in place, and that it does so selectively. Information that is rarely used fades; information that is repeatedly retrieved grows stronger. The result is a memory tuned less to the past than to the future, preserving what has proved useful and discarding what has not. Forgetting also appears to help us generalise. A child who has been bitten by one particular dog needs to remember that dogs can bite, not every detail of that afternoon. By losing the specific and keeping the general, the mind turns experience into knowledge that can be applied in new situations. Researchers who build artificial learning systems have observed something similar: programs that memorise their training examples too faithfully often perform badly when faced with anything unfamiliar. None of this means that every lapse is beneficial. The forgetting associated with illnesses such as dementia is a genuine loss, and the frustration of losing a word in mid-sentence is real enough. Nor does the research suggest that we should stop trying to learn. The point is rather that memory and forgetting work together, and that a system designed to remember everything would be less, not more, intelligent. There may be a personal dimension, too. People who cannot let go of painful memories, who replay old humiliations and quarrels in vivid detail, often suffer for it. Psychologists have long observed that the ability to allow certain experiences to lose their sharpness is part of recovering from them. Forgiveness, in this sense, may owe something to forgetting, or at least to a softening of the edges that time and a healthy brain provide. Cultures seem to have understood this long before the neuroscience. Oral traditions, which passed stories from one generation to the next without writing, never preserved every detail of the original telling; each storyteller kept the shape and the meaning of a tale while freely changing its surface. Far from being a weakness, this flexibility allowed stories to survive for centuries, adapting to new audiences while keeping what mattered. Written records, by contrast, preserve everything equally, the trivial alongside the essential, and leave it to the reader to decide which is which. Historians of the distant past often complain that they have too little evidence; those who study the recent past increasingly complain that they have far too much, and no time to read it. It is worth remembering, then, that the next time a name escapes us, our brains may simply be doing their job. In a world that offers us more information each day than our grandparents encountered in a year, the capacity to discard may be as valuable as the capacity to store. We have built machines that remember everything; perhaps the more human achievement is knowing what to forget.

I can translate the words literally, in which case the scene makes no sense; I can add a footnote explaining the pun, which kills the humour as effectively as explaining any joke; or I can invent a new pun in English that does a similar job. I have chosen the third option, which means that a few lines of the English novel are, strictly speaking, mine rather than the author's. Some translators would consider this a betrayal. I consider it the only form of faithfulness available. The problem extends far beyond wordplay. Languages divide the world differently. Portuguese has a word, saudade, for a kind of longing for something absent, perhaps lost for ever, that English can only approach through a phrase. Another insists that a good translation should feel slightly foreign, reminding readers that they are encountering a different culture rather than a version of their own. I find myself moving between the two, sometimes within a single page. Dialogue usually needs to sound natural, because people do not speak in translation; descriptions of a city or a meal can afford to keep a little of their original flavour. I read every chapter aloud before I send it to the publisher. A sentence can be accurate in every word and still be wrong, because its rhythm stumbles where the original flowed, or because it is half as long as the sentence it replaces and so loses the breathlessness the author intended. A program can tell me what a sentence says; it cannot yet tell me what the sentence is doing, or what it would cost to lose.
